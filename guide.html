Designing your portfolio as one brand experience

This site was built as an example for the students of Henry, the designer with Loid Forger’s rizz and Gromit’s quiet genius. Anya would give him eight Stellas. Gromit Forger is his alter ego from the Spider-Verse, and entirely fictional.

This site is never meant to be copied, not its code, not its look, and not its words. It is a generic example of the parts every portfolio needs, the site map, the visual structure, and the path a reviewer takes through the work. Study those decisions, then design a site that looks like you. This guide is not dogma. These are principles, highly recommended. Break one only when you can explain why.

1. One identity system

Your website, resume, PDF portfolio, and LinkedIn are one identity system. This is a brand experience project, not an exercise in self-expression. Every touch point should feel like the same designer, visually, experientially, and conceptually.

Your materials are communication first. They exist to deliver information clearly, and the way you handle that information is the proof of your skill. Restraint is not the absence of personality. Your personality lives in the system, in the grid, the type, and the consistency across everything you make.

One source of truth

Project names, job titles, dates, descriptions, and your voice stay identical everywhere. Only the format changes. Reviewers cross-check, and a date that differs between your resume and your LinkedIn reads as careless.

2. Tools and design literacy

Knowing how a website is built, the proper, traditional way, is part of your design literacy. HTML, CSS, JavaScript, wireframes, and Figma all matter. Use this project to raise your web design skills as far as you can.

Your portfolio website and your PDF are not just containers for your work. They are pieces of your portfolio in their own right, and they show your web design and page layout literacy as clearly as any project in them.

Use AI as your main tool

AI can build most of your site. You don’t have to write HTML and CSS by hand, but being able to read code helps you decide what needs to be done, in what way, and how to say it to the AI. Without that literacy, you hand your design integrity over to the tool.

Become fluent in your tools, knowing they will keep changing. Never be defined by a tool, and never let it design for you. If you do, you risk more than your professional ethos. You risk your career.

Templates and site builders

Readymag, Framer, Cargo, Wix, Squarespace, and WordPress are template and CMS models from an earlier era, and they matter less in this one. They are still worth studying, together with other designers’ portfolio sites, to learn what a strong portfolio looks like. They are also paid subscriptions, and the site disappears when you stop paying.

Browse with intent. Looking through existing designs without knowing what you are looking for, the way most people doomscroll Pinterest, is how copying starts. Copying is a plain violation of your own design ethos.

Build it on GitHub

Build your own site and host it on GitHub instead. GitHub Pages publishes a website straight from your files at no cost, with no subscription and no badge. Every change is saved as a version, so you can always go back. GitHub is the recommended home for this project, and this example site lives there too.

Owning your files also means owning your design. Nothing on your site is limited by what a template allows.

  1. Make an account at github.com.
  2. Create a repository. Make it public, because free GitHub Pages works with public repositories.
  3. Upload your files. Your home page must be named index.html and sit at the top level.
  4. Turn on Pages. In the repository, open Settings, then Pages. Choose “Deploy from a branch,” pick main, and save. A minute later your site appears at username.github.io.

Your domain

Give the site its own domain. Your name with .com is the first choice. If it is taken, .design is a strong second, and it tells a reviewer what you do before the page even opens.

The domain is the only thing you pay for, once a year. Register it with Porkbun, a registrar with fair prices and free privacy protection for your contact details. You can buy it any time, but connect it last, once your GitHub site is up.

  1. Connect your domain. In the same Pages settings, type your domain into “Custom domain” and save. Then in Porkbun, add the DNS records listed on GitHub’s custom domain page.
  2. Turn on “Enforce HTTPS” once the domain connects. DNS can take from a few minutes to a day, so don’t panic if it isn’t there right away.

3. Know your audience

You are an applicant

A portfolio site for an emerging designer is not a showcase of your personality. It is evidence of your capability and experience, made for people deciding whether to hire you for an entry-level role on their design team.

You are not a freelancer selling your services, and you are not a studio of one building a brand. You are applying to join a sophisticated, established design team. Write and design every page for that reader.

Show that you can do anything

A junior designer is asked to do whatever the team needs that week. A poster, a slide deck, a set of social posts, a packaging revision, a landing page, or a few app screens. Teams hire entry-level designers who are versatile, flexible, and adaptable. Your portfolio should prove you can take on any design work you are given, and do it well.

Information first

Every page should answer a reviewer’s question about your work. Save the rest for your personal Instagram. Whimsical script type, decorative embellishment, and that kick-ass bouldering or windsurfing photo have no place here.

Treat the About page as a formal cover letter. Say what role you are applying for, what you bring to a team, and when you are available. Leave a small space at the end for life outside design, a sentence or two that shows who you are.

Let your work speak for you. In the US, professional resumes and portfolio sites leave out personal photos, so reviewers focus on what you make and the hiring decision stays about your skill. Some countries still expect a photo on a resume, so check the norm wherever you apply. LinkedIn is the one exception, covered in chapter 9.

The first minute

Most reviewers decide in less than a minute whether to look closer. In that minute they want to see three things. Strong work, how you think, and how to reach you. Everything on this site follows from that. The work starts on the first screen. Every project opens with one sentence that says what it is. Email, resume, and LinkedIn are always one click away.

4. Choose the work

  • Eight to twelve projects. Reviewers won’t look at all of them, but the number still matters. It shows how much you can do. Every one has to be finished and strong.
  • Lead with your best. The first two decide whether anyone sees the rest.
  • Show range. Cover as many disciplines as you can, from brand identity, editorial, and poster design to packaging, spatial, information, interface, interaction, and motion design.
  • Cut anything you would have to apologize for.

Write your headline last, once you see what your strongest work has in common. “Graphic designer based in Atlanta” is true of hundreds of people. A clear point of view about your work is what a reviewer remembers. Keep it about the work, not about you.

5. Tell each story

Prove it’s yours

In the age of AI, anyone can produce a polished image in seconds. That makes it harder, and more important, to prove your design literacy. Show what only a designer can show. Your strategy, your intent, how you write a brief, and a process you can repeat as a method.

Human craftsmanship matters more than ever. The way your concept took shape and your visuals evolved is the evidence.

Choose that evidence carefully. No reviewer has time for a process book of 100 images. Most of each project should be strong final images, because the final work is the hero. Then show the few moments where the concept or the visuals changed direction. Sketches can be part of that story, but they are not the point. Keep it brief.

Get to the point

You are not writing a book. No one wants to read literature on someone else’s portfolio site. Make every sentence thoughtful, intentional, and precise. Then nail it.

Shape the story

Each project has its own page, because studios hire for thinking, not only for finished images. Every page tells its story the same way, so a reviewer always knows where to look.

  1. One sentence. What it is and why it matters. Many reviewers read only this.
  2. Discipline, format, tools, and role. The discipline of design it belongs to, the format (size, materials, and quantity), and the tools you used. Reviewers don’t need the name of the class. Add your role only for group work, and be specific about what you did.
  3. The work. The strongest image first, shown large. Final images should fill most of the page, so bring back more of them after the development.
  4. The brief. What you were asked to do.
  5. The idea. The insight that shaped your answer.
  6. The development. How the concept took shape and the visuals evolved, and the directions you let go of.
  7. The reflection. What worked, and what you would do differently.

The PDF tells the same story in less space. Keep the sentence, the details, the idea, and the strongest images. Cut the development to one or two moments, and leave the brief and reflection to the website.

6. The website

Your portfolio is a design project, and it deserves the same care as your work.

  • The work is the hero. The interface stays quiet so your projects can speak.
  • One system, everywhere. The same type, grid, and rhythm on every page.
  • Expressive in the work, restrained in the frame. Your voice comes through your projects, your writing, and thoughtful interaction, not through decoration.
  • Easy to navigate. No one should have to figure out your site before seeing your work.

The principles below apply to every touch point, your resume and PDF as much as your site. They are principles, not a style. Every one of them can be met with a look completely different from this example, and yours should be.

Grid

Choose a grid and commit to it. When every element starts on a column line, the page feels ordered before anyone can say why. How many columns, and how wide, is your decision. Then check it on a phone as carefully as on a laptop. A grid that holds on only one screen is not a system.

The grid never left digital design. The page did. Now that AI removes the technical cost, there is no excuse left for layouts that don’t come from a real system of proportion, measure, and hierarchy.

A short history. Bootstrap made a responsive 12-column grid standard on the web in 2012. Material Design defines columns, gutters, and margins for every screen width. CSS Grid has worked in every major browser since 2017, and subgrid and container queries followed. The tools have been ready for years.

Measure

Keep lines to a comfortable length, roughly 45 to 75 characters. Robert Bringhurst calls 66 ideal. Longer lines tire the eye, and shorter lines break the rhythm of reading.

Type

Use a small set of sizes and give each one a job. A new size needs a new job, not a new mood. Build hierarchy from size, position, and weight before adding anything else.

Space

Take every distance from one consistent scale. Few people will measure it, but everyone feels the calm and order. Space separates. Lines divide only what truly needs dividing.

Alignment

Decide on one system of alignment and hold it everywhere. Running text reads best flush left, because the eye has one place to start every line.

Color

Give every color a job. Color used for decoration competes with your work, and your work should always win.

Images

Show the work large and crop it with care. Compress your files, because a slow site loses reviewers before the first image appears.

Photography

Your photos are how people experience your design. Shoot every project in good light, in a considered setting, with a deliberate composition. A strong project in a weak photo looks like a weak project.

Motion

If you use motion, give it a purpose. One memorable movement is better than many. Always respect visitors who turn motion off.

7. The resume

The resume used to be the piece where a graphic designer proved their layout and typography, along with the business card. The business card is gone, and the portfolio you carried to interviews became a website. The resume stayed. Almost every application still asks for a PDF.

It’s information design

A resume is communication, not a place to show off your personality. Hierarchy, spacing, and type do the work. No personal photos, no script or playful type, and no extra colors. A quiet, well set resume proves more skill than a loud one.

  • One typeface family, two at most, and one accent color. A second face is fine for a wordmark of your name, the title of your whole system. Use it the same way on your site, resume, PDF, and LinkedIn banner. Keep it as live text, never outlined.
  • Plain section names. Experience, Education, and Skills. Let the typography carry the design.

Built for people and machines

Many companies run applications through an applicant tracking system, or ATS, that pulls the text out of your PDF before a person ever sees it. A designed layout is fine. Hidden structure is what breaks it.

  • Live text only. No outlined type, and no words set as images. A machine can’t read them.
  • Reading order beats column count. A narrow sidebar is fine, and the main body stays one column. Thread your text frames in the order they should be read.
  • Nothing important in headers or footers. Some systems skip them.

The paste test. Select all the text in your PDF and paste it into a plain text file. If it reads top to bottom like a sensible document, a machine can read it too.

8. The PDF portfolio

Many companies still ask for a PDF portfolio and a PDF resume. Build them from the same identity system and content as your site, so they never disagree.

Choose the page size with intent. Most reviewers read a PDF on a screen and some will print it, so the shape has to work in both places. A good starting point is 11 × 6.25 in.

Why these numbers. Almost every laptop and monitor is 16:9, so a 16:9 page fills the screen with no empty bands. The width of 11 in is the long side of a US letter sheet, so the page prints at full size in landscape on any office printer, with a little paper to spare above and below. Exact 16:9 at that width would be 6.1875 in, a number no one can measure, so the height rounds to 6.25 in, less than one percent off.

A PDF is read one page at a time. Give each project its own space, and make it obvious where one ends and the next begins. Number the pages. Put your name and web address on every page, because a PDF travels without the email it came in.

Build the file the way a professional would, with a column grid, hanglines (shared top lines for text and images), paragraph styles, parent pages, and automatic page numbers. How you build the file is part of what you are showing.

Let the images carry it. The full process lives on your site.

9. LinkedIn

LinkedIn is your public resume and the place where your professional network lives. If you don’t have an account, make one now.

  • Same content, different platform. Titles, dates, and descriptions match your resume word for word.
  • A photo and a background. Unlike your site, LinkedIn is about people meeting people, so a clean, professional photo belongs here. Make the background part of your identity system.
  • 500 connections by the end of the quarter. A low number reads as new to the field, or not serious about a career. Past 500, LinkedIn only shows “500+,” so that is the goal. Connect with designers, classmates, alumni, and people at companies you admire.
  • Join the SCAD Atlanta Graphic Design Alumni Network. Do it the day you open your account. Alumni are your strongest network. Ask them for feedback, and for an introduction when a company or a role interests you.
